The globe itself stands perfectly still and upright, but the liquid inside it is still spinning fast round the vertical axis of the globe, and snowflakes fill the whole globe as a storm of motion-blurred streaks, so thick that the miniature inside is half lost behind it. In image 1 the storm was painted in by hand. Photograph the same moment for real, at the same strength: every painted streak becomes a real snowflake or clump of snow smeared by motion blur, the painted haze becomes real powder snow turning in the liquid, and nothing looks painted any more.

AS THICK AS IMAGE 1. This is the one thing that must not change. Wherever the storm covers the miniature in image 1, it covers it in the result, just as thickly. Do not clear the storm away from the cottage or the tree, do not thin it, do not calm it, and do not reveal anything that image 1 hides. The first thing the eye sees is a globe full of whirling snow; only then does it find the miniature inside, dim and veiled, like a house seen through a blizzard at night.

THE STORM IN THE LIQUID. Keep the number of streaks, their directions, their lengths and their three sizes as they are in image 1. Every particle is drawn out along its path by its speed; none hangs still and sharp. All the streaks follow one coherent rotation round the vertical axis of the globe: they run in long shallow arcs that bend with the curve of the glass, nearly level across the middle of the globe and curving round at its left and right edges, so the whole body of liquid is seen to turn, a vortex viewed from the side. There are three depths. Close against the front glass: a few large, long, soft, out-of-focus streaks. At the distance of the cottage and the tree: a great many fine streaks with crisp edges, each one a short curved dash, crossing in front of the cottage, the tree and the lamppost. Behind the miniature: the far side of the swirl, a turning haze of very fine faint streaks. Thicker bands of storm wind round the globe like the arms of a spiral, with thinner gaps between them. The storm fills the whole globe, from the front glass to the back glass and from the ground to the very top of the dome, right out to the glass on every side, with no calm, dark, empty patch anywhere in the liquid.

THE PARTICLES. Snow: a blizzard, close to a white-out. Many thousands of flakes, from fine dust to fat clumps, every one a white streak. Between and behind them the liquid is milky with powder snow. The shaking has torn the loose snow off the ground of the miniature: a thick cloud of white powder boils up from the bottom of the globe and is drawn up into the rotation, so the lowest third of the liquid is almost solid white. The flakes are cold white and blue-white where the evening light catches them and turn warm gold where they pass the lights of the miniature.

WHAT STILL SHOWS OF THE MINIATURE. Only this, and only dimly, through the gaps between the streaks: the lit gable of the cottage with the string lights along its eaves, the glow of its windows and the wreath on its door, the lamppost as a warm blur, the dark trunk of the tree and patches of its snow-laden crown with the star above it. The fence, the path, the pond, the foot of the tree and the foot of the cottage walls are buried in the churned-up snow and cannot be seen at all; the far roof and the chimney are lost in white. What does show stands exactly where it stands in image 1 and is not motion-blurred itself, but it is pale and low in contrast behind the storm. Light from inside the globe carries through the storm: the string lights on the eaves and in the tree, the star, the windows and the lamppost burn as soft warm amber halos, and the flakes that pass close to them light up gold, so the heart of the white storm glows warm against the cold blue of the glass.

MOTION BLUR ON THE PARTICLES ONLY. Only what the liquid carries is streaked. The miniature, the glass, the frost on the glass, the base, the surface and the background have no motion blur at all. The camera has not moved and the globe has not moved.
